To validate the correctness and evaluate the performance of the implemented LDPC decoder, a two-part experimental approach was adopted.

First, a series of preliminary tests were conducted in which the length of the error vector \(\mathbf{E}\) was systematically varied. These tests served to verify the functional correctness of the decoder implementation by confirming its ability to correctly decode received codewords with controlled levels of error. This step ensured that the decoder operated as expected before proceeding to more complex performance evaluations~\cite{Liva2012, Fossorier2004}.

Subsequently, a Monte Carlo simulation framework was employed to model transmission over an additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) channel. Monte Carlo simulations are a statistical technique where the system is simulated numerous times with random noise realizations added according to the noise model, allowing estimation of performance metrics such as bit error rate (BER) or block error rate (BLER) by averaging over many trials~\cite{Proakis2001, Kim2015}. 

In our simulations, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the form of \(E_b/N_0\) was varied over the range from \(-6\, \mathrm{dB}\) to \(-4\, \mathrm{dB}\), covering conditions around the decoder's expected threshold region. The influence of the maximum number of decoding iterations on the block error rate (BLER)  \todo{why are we measuring the BLER ?} was also examined, demonstrating the trade-off between decoding complexity (iterations) and error correction performance.

Noise was added to transmitted codewords to emulate realistic AWGN channel conditions. The decoder was evaluated using each of the four implemented algorithms: Sum-Product Algorithm (SPA), Min-Sum (MS), Offset Min-Sum (OMS), and Normalized Min-Sum (NMS)~\cite{Ryan2009, Richardson2001}.

The Monte Carlo simulation process statistically estimates the probability of decoding errors by comparing decoded outputs to known transmitted bits across many trials, allowing for robust empirical performance evaluation.

The detailed comparative analysis of the decoding algorithms under these noise and iteration parameters, including error correction capability and computational considerations, is presented in the subsequent Results and Analysis chapter.

This section presents the decoding complexity comparison among the four implemented decoding algorithms: Sum-Product Algorithm (SPA), Min-Sum (MS), Offset Min-Sum (OMS), and Normalized Min-Sum (NMS). The analysis is based on graphical results obtained from simulation data, including individual complexity curves for each algorithm as well as a combined plot for direct side-by-side comparison.

The individual graphs depict the number of iterations required for convergence, computational effort (e.g., measured by average number of message updates or processing time), and the corresponding block error rate (BLER) performance for each algorithm under tested conditions. The SPA graph generally shows the highest computational complexity per iteration but also exhibits the best error-correction performance, requiring fewer iterations to reach a target BLER compared to approximated algorithms.

The MS algorithm graph reveals significantly reduced complexity due to its simplified check-node update, yet it requires more iterations to achieve comparable BLER levels, reflecting a trade-off between computational savings and decoding efficiency. OMS and NMS graphs illustrate improvements over pure MS by introducing correction factors—offset and normalization respectively—that strike a balance between complexity and decoding accuracy. OMS typically outperforms MS in BLER with only a moderate increase in complexity, while NMS can closely approach SPA performance with below-SPA computational demands.

The combined graph highlights these differences clearly, enabling direct visual evaluation: SPA achieves the best error performance per iteration but at highest computational cost; MS offers lowest complexity but at the expense of performance; OMS and NMS occupy intermediate points, making them attractive options where complexity constraints exist but near-optimal decoding is desired.

\subsection{Overall Performance and Recommendations}

From the comparative results, it can be concluded that the choice of decoding algorithm depends on the targeted application’s performance and resource requirements:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SPA} is preferable in scenarios where decoding accuracy is paramount and computational resources (processing power, energy) are sufficient. Examples include offline data decoding or high-reliability communication links.
    
    \item \textbf{MS} is suitable for resource-constrained environments where decoding complexity must be minimized and some performance degradation is acceptable, such as low-power devices or real-time systems with strict latency limits.
    
    \item \textbf{OMS} offers a good compromise, improving performance compared to MS with a minimal complexity increase. Suitable for systems requiring a balance between performance and efficiency.
    
    \item \textbf{NMS} generally approaches SPA performance while incurring significantly less complexity, making it an excellent candidate for most practical applications where near-optimal performance is desired without excessive resource consumption.
\end{itemize}

In summary, the graphical and quantitative analysis confirms that OMS and NMS decoding algorithms provide efficient alternatives to SPA, enabling adaptable decoder designs tailored to diverse deployment constraints.

Further exploration of the trade-offs between decoding latency, power consumption, and error performance may guide deployment decisions in specific system architectures.

\subsection{Decoding speed and complexity.}
\label{subsec:decoding_speed_complexity}

An important metric in evaluating LDPC decoding algorithms is the decoding speed or latency, which directly impacts system throughput and real-time processing capabilities. Table~\ref{tab:decoding_speed} summarizes the measured decoding latencies at a reference signal-to-noise ratio (SNR$_\text{ref}$), along with the relative speedup of each algorithm compared to the Sum-Product Algorithm (SPA), which serves as the baseline.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Decoding Latency and Speedup at Reference SNR}
\label{tab:decoding_speed}
\begin{tabular}{l r r}
\toprule
Algorithm & Latency@SNR$_\text{ref}$ [$\mu$s] & Speedup vs SPA \\
\midrule
SPA & 17915.4 & 1.00 \\
Min-Sum (MS)  & 8228.7 & 2.18 \\
Offset Min-Sum (OMS) & 8327.6 & 2.15 \\
Normalized Min-Sum (NMS) & 6767.5 & 2.65 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The results indicate that the SPA, while delivering the most accurate decoding, incurs the highest latency due to its complex check-node computations involving nonlinear functions. The MS algorithm approximately halves the latency compared to SPA by using simplified minimum and sign computations, trading off some decoding accuracy for speed.

Both OMS and NMS introduce modest computational overhead relative to MS because of the offset and normalization corrections, respectively. However, these overheads are minimal and latency remains around twice as fast as SPA. Notably, NMS achieves the best speedup (2.65× faster than SPA) among the approximated algorithms, aligning well with its balance between complexity and decoding performance observed earlier.

These latency measurements reinforce the practical considerations when selecting a decoding algorithm: SPA is most suitable where decoding accuracy is critical and processing resources are plentiful; NMS provides a strong alternative offering considerably reduced latency with minimal performance loss; MS and OMS suit scenarios with stricter speed or power constraints but can tolerate slightly degraded decoding.

Therefore, the presented latency and speedup data complement the BER and BLER performance results to guide algorithm choice tailored to specific application requirements.


\section{Comparative Analysis with Existing Implementation}
\label{sec:comparative_analysis}

An important aspect of this work is the comparison between the newly implemented LDPC decoding algorithms (SPA, MS, OMS, and NMS) and the legacy decoder implementation provided by the Open Air Interface (OAI) framework.

The original OAI decoder was designed primarily as a hard-decision decoder, optimized for simplicity and integration within the broader 5G software ecosystem. Its decoding process operated on binary hard decisions without exploiting soft information from the channel, limiting error correction performance in noisy environments. Additionally, the legacy implementation employed a relatively fixed decoding schedule and fewer algorithmic options.

In contrast, the new implementation supports full soft-decision decoding with log-likelihood ratios (LLRs) as input, enabling more precise reliability information to be processed during decoding. This facilitates the use of advanced iterative message-passing algorithms such as SPA, OMS, and NMS, which leverage soft information to significantly improve error-correction capability.

Furthermore, the modular software architecture of the new decoder allows configurable decoding schedules (layered or flooding), selection among multiple decoding algorithms, and flexible control over iteration limits and stopping criteria. This flexibility was not present in the original OAI decoder, which had a more rigid design.

From a computational complexity perspective, the new implementation trades additional processing and memory requirements due to soft-decision operations and more sophisticated algorithms for considerable gains in decoding performance, especially under low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions. The original OAI decoder is more resource-efficient with its simpler hard-decision logic but exhibits suboptimal performance for demanding channel conditions.

Overall, the new decoder implementation provides a comprehensive upgrade in both functionality and performance, addressing limitations observed in the OAI legacy version. By supporting multiple advanced algorithms and soft-decision inputs, it offers enhanced robustness and adaptability for future wireless communication scenarios.

Experimental results in the previous chapter quantitatively demonstrate these improvements, highlighting the benefits of moving beyond hard-decision decoding to sophisticated soft-decision iterative algorithms.

At first glance, the OAI legacy decoder exhibits an extraordinary speed advantage, operating approximately 86 times faster than even the fastest newly implemented algorithm (NMS). This substantial difference arises primarily due to fundamental architectural and algorithmic distinctions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hard-decision vs. Soft-decision decoding:} The OAI decoder employs hard-decision decoding, which operates on binary values without processing soft information. This allows significantly simpler computations—primarily bit-level operations such as XOR—in stark contrast to the real-valued iterative message-passing computations employed by SPA, MS, OMS, and NMS~\cite{oai, Ryan2009}.
    
    \item \textbf{Simplified update rules:} The legacy decoder’s iteration steps involve less complex logic, abandoning nonlinear functions and likelihood computations. This reduces both computational burden and memory requirements drastically~\cite{Larsson-Edefors2014}.
    
    \item \textbf{Software implementation trade-offs:} The new decoder prototypes target modularity, extensibility, and advanced decoding algorithms, incurring overheads due to flexible scheduling, soft-value processing, and algorithm selection mechanisms. In contrast, OAI’s implementation is optimized for real-time operation in constrained environments, often at the cost of decoding performance~\cite{Roth2015}.
\end{itemize}

While the OAI decoder excels in speed due to its simplicity, it inherently sacrifices error correction capability, especially at low SNR regimes where soft information and complex decoding algorithms yield substantial gains. The newly implemented soft-decision decoders deliver superior error rate performance, supporting more robust communications in challenging scenarios, albeit at higher computational cost.

Thus, the performance-versus-complexity trade-off is evident: the OAI legacy decoder suits scenarios with stringent latency and resource constraints, whereas the advanced soft-decision decoders developed herein enable enhanced reliability where processing power permits.
