\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

To evaluate the performance of the implemented \gls{ldpc} decoder, a series of Monte Carlo simulations were carried out. 
The simulations emulate transmission over an \gls{awgn} channel, which is widely used as a baseline channel model for error-control coding analysis.

Each transmitted codeword $x \in \{-1, +1\}^N$ (\gls{bpsk} modulated) was corrupted by Gaussian noise, resulting in the received vector

$$y = x + n, n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2\mathcal{I})$$

where $\sigma^2$ is the noise variance determined by the desired \gls{snr}. The cannel \glspl{llr} were then computed as

$$L_{ch}(y_i) = \frac{2}{\sigma^2}y_i$$

and provided as input to the decoder.

The \gls{snr} was parameterized in terms of $E_b/N_0$ and swept across the range from -6dB to -4dB. For each \gls{snr} point, sufficient Monte Carlo trials were performed to obtain statistically meaningful results, The primary performance metric use was the \gls{bler}, defined as

$$BLER = \frac{N_{err}}{N_{tot}},$$

where $N_{err}$ denotes the number of incorrectly decoded codeblocks and $N_{tot}$ the total number of transmitted codeblocks at a given \gls{snr}.

The simulations were repeated for all four implemented decoding algorithms — \gls{spa}, \gls{msa}, \gls{oms}, and \gls{nms} — under otherwise identical conditions. 
This ensures that performance differences can be attributed directly to the choice of decoding algorithm rather than simulation parameters.

\section{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:performance_evaluation}

To assess the implemented decoder, two aspects were considered: error-correction performance and decoding speed. 
The evaluation compares the four implemented algorithms: \gls{spa}, \gls{msa}, \gls{oms}, and \gls{nms} - under identical simulation conditions.

As expected, SPA achieves the best performance since it implements the full belief-propagation rules without approximation.

MS exhibits a noticeable performance degradation compared to SPA due to its simplification of the check-node update to a minimum operation.

OMS and NMS significantly close the gap to SPA by correcting for the bias introduced by plain Min-Sum. OMS applies a constant offset to the minimum magnitude, whereas NMS applies a scaling factor; both methods yield BLER curves close to SPA with only minor penalties.

At very low SNR values, differences between the algorithms are less pronounced, while near the waterfall region the performance ordering becomes clear.

SPA requires more expensive operations $tanh, tanh^{-1}$ and is the slowest.

MS is the fastest due to its simple minimum and sign operations, at the cost of performance.

OMS and NMS introduce only a small overhead compared to MS (an addition or multiplication per check-node update), while achieving much better error-correction capability.

Overall, OMS and NMS provide the best trade-off between performance and speed.
